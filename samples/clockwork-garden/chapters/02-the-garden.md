# The Garden

The address on the back of the envelope led her to the edge of the city, where the streets gave up their names and the houses grew further apart, as if embarrassed to be seen together. At the end of a lane that did not appear on any map she owned stood a wall, and in the wall, a gate, and in the gate, a lock shaped exactly like the curling teeth of her key.

She should have been afraid. She told herself this quite firmly as she slid the key home and felt it catch, and turned it, and heard — from somewhere beyond the wall — a sound like a thousand tiny throats clearing at once.

A small brass plate was riveted to the gate, its letters worn but legible:

::: sign
Mind the hour. The garden remembers.
:::

The gate swung open.

* * *

It was not a garden so much as a *mechanism* that had decided, at some point, to become beautiful.

Roses unfurled on hinges of polished brass, their petals chiming faintly as they opened. Tulips stood in neat brass rows, each one ticking — actually ticking, like a pocket watch — and where an ordinary garden would have had bees, this one had small clockwork things that hummed between the blooms on wings of beaten silver.

At the centre of it all stood a sundial that cast no shadow, and beside the sundial stood a man who was quite plainly not alive, and had not been for some time, and who turned to her nonetheless and said, in a voice like a music box winding down:

"You're late. We've been losing time for years."

Margaret, who had spent her whole life being told she was too practical for her own good, discovered in that moment exactly how much good practicality did a person when a dead man complained to her about the hour.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I came as fast as I could."

The man — or the thing that remembered being a man — inclined his head. "They always do," he said. "And it is never fast enough. Come. There is a great deal to wind, and the frost is early this year."
