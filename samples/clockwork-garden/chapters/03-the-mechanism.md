# The Mechanism

By the third evening, Margaret had learned the garden's grammar. You wound the roses clockwise and the tulips counter; you never, ever touched the white lilies near the eastern wall, which kept a different time entirely and resented interruption; and you listened, always, for the one sound a healthy garden should never make — silence.

Silence meant something had stopped.

And on the third evening, the lilies fell silent.

* * *

"It's the central spring," said Ashby — for that was the dead man's name, she had learned, Cornelius Ashby, who had built the garden and then, rather than leave it, become a part of it. "It has wound down before. But never this far. Never this fast."

Margaret knelt by the sundial and pressed her ear to the cold brass. Far below, deep in the earth where the roots of impossible things tangled together, she could hear it: a vast slow heartbeat, faltering.

*Tick.*

A long pause.

*Tick.*

"How long," she asked, "since it last beat true?"

Ashby was quiet for a moment, and when he spoke, the music had gone out of his voice. "Since the day I died," he said. "I have been holding it together with want alone, Margaret, and want is a poor sort of clockwork. It needs hands. It needs someone who fixes things." He looked at her, and something in his ruined face was almost gentle. "It needs you to decide whether some things are worth more than the time they cost."

Margaret looked at the key in her palm, warm now, warmer than it had any right to be, ticking faintly in time with the heart beneath the garden.

By the sundial lay Ashby's old notebook, fallen open to a page in his cramped, hopeful hand.

The central spring is not a thing you fix. It is a thing you forgive.

Then she rolled up her sleeves, and opened her grandfather's tin of tools, and got to work.
