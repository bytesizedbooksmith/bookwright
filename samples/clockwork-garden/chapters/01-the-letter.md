# The Letter

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Margaret would later decide was the most ordinary day the universe could have chosen for something so impossible. It came without a stamp, without a postmark, and without any explanation for how it had found its way beneath her door in a building where the lock had not worked since the war.

She turned it over twice. The paper was heavy and faintly warm, as though it had been resting against someone's heart. On the front, in ink the colour of old copper, was written a single line: *To the girl who fixes things.*

That, at least, was accurate.

Margaret fixed things. Toasters, mostly, and the occasional radio brought to her by neighbours who could not afford the shop on the corner. She had a small table by the window and a tin of tools that had belonged to her grandfather, and she was, by general agreement, the only person on Carlow Street who could make a broken clock keep time again.

* * *

Inside the envelope was a key.

It was brass, and heavier than it looked, with teeth cut in a pattern she had never seen before — not straight, not even, but curling, like the petals of a flower folding shut at dusk. And there was a note, three lines only, in that same copper ink:

::: letter
The garden is yours now. It has been waiting longer than you have been alive.

Wind it gently. It frightens easily.

[— C. A.]{.signature}
:::

Margaret read it four times. Then she put on her coat.
